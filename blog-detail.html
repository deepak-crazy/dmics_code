Disaster Risk in India 2025: Key Lessons from a Year of Extremes

The year 2025 marked a defining chapter in India’s disaster risk landscape. Extreme weather events occurred with unprecedented frequency and geographic spread, affecting nearly every region of the country. Floods, heatwaves, cloudbursts, cyclones, landslides, lightning, and urban inundation were no longer isolated incidents but part of a persistent and intensifying pattern of risk.

India’s experience in 2025 offers critical lessons on the interplay between climate change, exposure, vulnerability, and preparedness, underscoring the urgent need to re-think disaster risk reduction (DRR) in the context of a warming world.

A Year Dominated by Extreme Weather

Data compiled by the Centre for Science and Environment (CSE) reveals that India experienced extreme weather events on 331 of 334 days in 2025 — nearly 99% of the year. This relentless exposure affected agriculture, infrastructure, livelihoods, and public safety across states.

Extreme weather events resulted in over 4,400 reported fatalities nationwide, marking a nearly 50% increase compared to 2022, while crop damage escalated dramatically to about 17.4 million hectares, almost nine times higher than losses recorded just three years earlier. States such as Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Maharashtra, and Assam consistently reported the highest mortality and damage figures, reflecting both hazard intensity and population exposure.

The persistence of disasters throughout the year signals a clear shift away from seasonal risk toward year-round climate volatility, challenging traditional assumptions that disasters are confined to specific months or regions.

Major Disaster Patterns Observed in 2025

i. Floods and Cloudbursts in the Himalayan Region

Several Himalayan states witnessed repeated cloudbursts and flash floods, particularly in Jammu & Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand. Districts such as Kishtwar, Kullu, Mandi, Uttarkashi, and Chamoli faced sudden river surges and landslides destroying settlements and pilgrimage routes. Rising temperatures combined with fragile geology and infrastructure development have made the Himalayas one of the country’s most climate-sensitive regions.

ii. Large-Scale Flooding in the Plains

Monsoon flooding in the plains accounted for nearly three-quarters of disaster-related fatalities in 2025. Major rivers including the Ganga, Brahmaputra, Yamuna, Ghaggar, and Godavari crossed danger levels multiple times. Urban inundation paralyzed cities like Patna, Guwahati, Delhi, and Ahmedabad. Maharashtra alone recorded over 8.4 million hectares of crop losses.

iii. Cyclones and Coastal Vulnerability

The Bay of Bengal generated frequent cyclones impacting Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, West Bengal, and Tamil Nadu. While early warning systems drastically curtailed mortality, economic losses to fisheries, coastal agriculture, and infrastructure were extensive.

iv. Heatwaves, Lightning, and Localised Extremes

Heatwaves struck large parts of central and northern India, while urban heat stress severely impacted Delhi, Nagpur, and Hyderabad. Lightning emerged as one of the deadliest weather hazards, claiming over 1,300 lives across eastern UP, Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha, and Chhattisgarh.

Why Disaster Risk Is Increasing

India’s disaster losses in 2025 were driven not only by hazards but also by rapidly escalating exposure:

  • Rapid urbanisation without climate-sensitive drainage planning
  • Encroachment of natural floodplains, wetlands, and fragile hill slopes
  • Infrastructure designed for historical climate conditions rather than extremes
  • Socio-economic vulnerabilities among informal settlements and smallholder farmers

Key Lessons from 2025

a) Early Warning Must Reach the Last Mile

Warning systems must be people-centric, localised, and multilingual, especially for rapid cloudbursts and lightning strikes.

b) Infrastructure Must Be Climate-Resilient

Urban drainage, bridges, roads, and embankments must be designed to withstand future climate volatility rather than historical medians.

c) Risk-Informed Development Is Non-Negotiable

DRR must be integrated into land-use zoning, municipal masterplans, and public capital allocations.

d) Community Capacity Saves Lives

Empowered local volunteers, Panchayati Raj Institutions, and resident welfare bodies remain the most agile frontline responders.

e) Financing Resilience Is Critical

Scaling up risk-transfer mechanisms, catastrophe insurance, and parametric climate relief funds.

Looking Ahead: From Response to Risk Reduction

The events of 2025 reinforce a central truth: disasters are no longer rare shocks — they are systemic risks. India’s progress in disaster response must now be matched with equal commitment to prevention, preparedness, and resilience building, aligned with the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction and national climate commitments.
